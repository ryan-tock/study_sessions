CORNERSTONE - DESIGN I.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nEquivalent with EPIC151,<br/>(I, II, S) Design I teaches students how to solve open-ended problems in a hands-on manner using critical thinking and workplace skills. Students work in multidisciplinary teams to learn through doing, with emphasis on defining and diagnosing the problem through a holistic lens of technology, people and culture. Students follow a user-centered design methodology throughout the process, seeking to understand a problem from multiple perspectives before attempting to solve it. Students learn and apply specific skills throughout the semester, including: communication (written, oral, graphical), project management, concept visualization, critical thinking, effective teamwork, as well as building and iterating solutions.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS151">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>1. Identify, breakdown, and define open-ended problems.</li><li>2. Research the context and background of problems and solutions, including user needs and technical requirements, through scholarly and authoritative sources, and stakeholder input.</li><li>3. Design solutions through a cycle of testing, refining, iterating, and feedback.</li><li>4. Equitably contribute to team efforts from start to end on a collaborative project, and participate in learning activities and coaching activities in the team.</li><li>5. Apply common workplace practices, tools and software in a semester-long team project, including project planning tools, team management tools, tools to generate solution alternatives, decision analysis methods, risk analysis methods, and value proposition analysis/baseline comparison.</li><li>6. Present technical ideas and solutions graphically, orally, written, and through prototype demonstrations</li><li>7. Visually depict ideas to teammates, supervisors, and stakeholders through the use of field sketching for the purposes of communication as well as idea development and development through iteration.</li><li>8. Model and communicate formalized design ideas through the use of standardized engineering graphics conventions as applied to engineering sketching and computer-aided design/solid modeling software</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS155. AUTOCAD BASICS.  1.0 Semester Hr.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\n(I, II) This course explores the two- and three-dimensional viewing and construction capabilities of AutoCAD. Students will learn to use AutoCAD for modeling (2D line drawing, 3D construction, Rendering, Part Assembly) and will develop techniques to improve speed and accuracy. The AutoCAD certification exam will not be offered as part of this course; however, the professor will provide instructions on accessing certification options, which generally have their own fees associated with them. 3 hours lab; 1 semester hour.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS156">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>1- Identify the components of the AutoCAD user interface and basic CAD terminology.</li><li>2- Apply basic concepts to develop construction (drawing) techniques.</li><li>3- Manipulate drawings through editing and plotting techniques.</li><li>4- Apply geometric construction and produce 2D Orthographic Projections.</li><li>5 - Interpret dimensions and demonstrate dimensioning concepts and techniques.</li><li>6- Reuse existing content and become familiar with the use of Blocks.</li><li>7- Explore the three-dimensional viewing and construction capabilities of AutoCAD.</li><li>8- Create and edit 3D Models from 2D profiles. Extract 2D views from a 3D model for detail drafting.</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS157.  SOLIDWORKS BASICS (FOR CERTIFICATION).  1.0 Semester Hr.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\n(I, II) Students will become familiar and confident with Solidworks CAD program and be able to use most of the basic functions well, including Parts, Assemblies, and Drawing Layouts.   The Associate-level certification exam will be offered at the end of the course, and while there are no guarantees for students becoming certified, students will have gained the necessary skills to try.  3 hours lab; 1 semester hour.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS157">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>1- Identify the components of the Solidworks user interface and basic CAD terminology and approaches.</li><li>2- Apply basic solid modeling concepts and use the basic part modeling functionality of Solidworks software.</li><li>3 - Develop defined and valid advanced 2 D sketch profiles in Solidworks for use in 3D operations and features.</li><li>4- Apply basic technical drawing concepts to interpret technical drawings for part modeling.</li><li>5 - Demonstrate dimensioning concepts and techniques by interpreting and creating properly annotated technical drawings.</li><li>6 - Identify and apply the techniques of 3D models such as revolve, sweep, and loft features.</li><li>7 - Identify geometric relations and functions of an assembly design to virtually assembly a set of parts into an assembly.</li><li>8 -Extract two-dimensional views from a three-dimensional model and assembly for detail drafting</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS198. INTRODUCTION TO DESIGN ENGINEERING.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nGood design is tuned to a purpose, engages users and rewards their attention with deeper meaning and insight. This course introduces the foundations of user experience design in the context of sociotechnical design engineering. Students examine the influences of human psychology, culture, cognition and perception on user experience design, establish a strong understanding of good design principles and their effective application. Students develop and hone an understanding of user-centered and user experience design concepts through an iterative design process.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS200">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>Establish a fundamental understanding of the phases of the user experience design cycle.</li><li>Understand the value in user-centered perspectives and the implications of human perception and cognition for user experience and interaction design.</li><li>Explore root causes for strengths and weaknesses of designs and provide suggestions of how to improve design for intended user.</li><li>Apply and evaluate usability testing as a form of design iteration and improvement.</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS205.  PROGRAMMING CONCEPTS AND ENGINEERING ANALYSIS.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\n(I,II) This course provides an introduction to techniques of scientific computation that are utilized for engineering analysis, with the software package MATLAB as the primary computational platform. The course focuses on methods data analysis and programming, along with numerical solutions to algebraic and differential equations. Engineering applications are used as examples throughout the course. 3 hours lecture; 3 semester hours.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS205">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n</div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS210.  PHYSICAL PROTOTYPING.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nWhat makes a design &quot;work&quot;? How can design ideas become a reality? This course explores these questions by focusing on how physical prototypes help design engineers explore and communicate ideas. Students gain a better understanding of the process by which they most effectively create design artifacts. Through a progressive series of design, creation, critique and reflection cycles, students complete multiple design challenges. These challenges culminate in systems integration while using data to inform their design decisions. 5 studio hours; 3 semester hours. Prerequisites: HASS100 &amp; ENDS151 or HNRS115 or HNRS120. Co-requisites: EDNS200, PHGN200.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS210">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>Design engineering solutions that enhance the user experience through solicitation and appropriate use of feedback.</li><li>Prototype to explore ideas and test concepts through iterative data-driven decision making.</li><li>Create artifacts using a range of fabrication techniques and iterations that take appropriate levels of fidelity into consideration.</li><li>Communicate with others, presenting ideas and solutions in ways that are appropriate for the occasion and audience.</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS220.  PROBLEM FRAMING &amp; STAKEHOLDER ENGAGEMENT.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nHow should design engineers frame problems and identify opportunities for change within sociotechnical systems? Students learn design methods to frame problems at multiple levels and scales, from the individual end user to high-level regulatory structures. Students actively engage with diverse stakeholders throughout the process to explore problem spaces, identify opportunities for design interventions, and examine potential avenues for solutions. Thematic areas such as sustainability, regenerative development, socioecological systems, and community engagement will drive students to look beyond the technical dimensions of problems to incorporate social, regulatory and location speci\xef\xac\x81c experiences into their problem framing methods. Prerequisites: EDNS151, HASS100.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS220">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>Describe social and technical interconnections of real-world design practice by exploring organizational contexts and stakeholder perspectives.</li><li>Apply sociotechnical, environmental and economic reasoning to consider values in the context of design systems thinking.</li><li>Identify and interpret ethical implications of designs.</li><li>Practice empathy and listening to better understand stakeholder needs and concerns.</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS251.  CORNERSTONE DESIGN II.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nEquivalent with EPIC251,<br/>Design II builds on the design process introduced in Design I, which focuses on open-ended problem solving in which students integrate teamwork and communications with the use of design techniques, business tools, and computer software to solve engineering problems. Student project teams now work with real-world clients while infusing introductory business skills including Agile project management tools, time-value of money and financial project justifications to address client needs. Computer applications emphasize data analytics. Teams build team dynamics and ensure satisfaction of client needs through team meetings and sprint reviews. The course emphasizes oral, visual, and written technical communications techniques introduced in Design I. 2 hours lecture, 3 hours lab; 3 semester hours. Prerequisite: EDNS151, EDNS155, HNRS115, or HNRS120.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS251">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>1. INDEPENDENT STUDY.  1-6 Semester Hr.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nEquivalent with EPIC299A,<br/>(I, II) Individual research or special problem projects supervised by a faculty member, also, when a student and instructor agree on a subject matter, content, and credit hours. HUMAN-CENTERED PROBLEM DEFINITION.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\n(I, II) This class will equip students with the knowledge, skills and attitudes needed to identify, define, and begin solving real problems for real people, within the socio-technical ambiguity that surrounds all engineering problems. The course will focus on problems faced in everyday life, by people from different backgrounds and in different circumstances, so that students will be able to rise to the occasion presented by future workplace challenges. By the end of this course, students will be able to recognize design problems around them, determine whether they are worth solving, and employ a suite of tools to create multiple solutions. The follow up course --&quot;Design for People&quot; -- will enable students to take the best solutions to the prototype phase. 3 hours lecture; 3 semester hours.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS301">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n</div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS310.  SYSTEMS MODELING &amp; DESIGN.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nComplex problems in areas of healthcare, transportation, energy distribution, and communication require integrative solutions spanning sociotechnical and environmental perspectives. In this course, students explore systems of thinking as a holistic approach to solving complex problems. Students engage with systems thinking in a way that recognizes the \'whole\' of the problem through analyzing interrelationships, attributes and effects. Students apply systems thinking perspectives to a complex sociotechnical problem, describe the problem through modeling techniques, design a holistic solution and improve upon the solution through justification and systems thinking approaches. Prerequisites: EDNS210, EDNS220. Co-requisites: MATH225.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS310">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>Establish a fundamental understanding of systems thinking terminology, methods, practices and tools.</li><li>Frame complex technical systems models using quantitative and qualitative methods.</li><li>Use a holistic systems thinking approach to understand a complex problem and design a solution.</li><li>Apply systems modeling and integration techniques to evaluate and optimize design solutions.</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS315.  ENGINEERING FOR SOCIAL AND ENVIRONMENTAL RESPONSIBILITY.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\n(WI) This course explores how engineers think about and practice environmental and social responsibility, and critically analyzes codes of ethics before moving to a deeper focus on macroethical topics with direct relevance to engineering practice, environmental sustainability, social and environmental justice, social entrepreneurship, corporate social responsibility, and engagement with the public. These macroethical issues are examined through a variety of historical and contemporary case studies and a broad range of technologies. Prerequisite: HASS100 and EDNS151. 3 hours lecture; 3 semester hours.<br/>\n<p class="notinpdf coursedraw-hidden"><a href="/search/?search=EDNS315">View Course Learning Outcomes</a></p>\n<p class="courseblockextra noindent"><div class="bubble-hidden"><h6 class="toggle">View Course Learning Outcomes</h6><ul><li>Identify and connect key moments in the history of engineering professions related to environmental and social responsibilities with current engineering challenges, particularly from the 20th century through current day, and how the idea of \xe2\x80\x9cresponsibility\xe2\x80\x9d in the engineering profession has changed throughout this history</li><li>Define key terms that relate the engineering professions\xe2\x80\x99 environmental and social responsibilities</li><li>Identify stakeholders in engineering projects, and analyze their roles, perspectives, and implications in environmental and social responsibility from various sectors and disciplines</li><li>Critique pervasive engineering mindsets and their relationship to engineers\xe2\x80\x99 responsibilities; where these attitudes and approaches are first established and subsequently reinforced through educational and professional practice</li><li>Create and develop persuasive arguments for practical steps to promote environmental and social responsibility in engineering projects, using professional tools for risk analysis, life cycle assessment, and cost/benefit while recognizing the limitations of any numerical simplification</li></ul></div></p></div><div class="courseblock">\n<p class="courseblocktitle"><strong>EDNS320.  ENGINEERING JUDGMENT.  3.0 Semester Hrs.</strong></p>\n<p class="courseblockdesc">\n</p>\nNavigating real-world engineering problems demands knowing when and how to apply distinct forms of expertise as well as the limitations of that expertise. We call this engineering judgment.
